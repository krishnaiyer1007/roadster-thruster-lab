What happens

The car drives at the row, then four underbody nozzles fire straight down for about half a second. That kicks it upward at roughly 1.5 g on top of gravity. After the burn it is a projectile: it rises, peaks and falls. Just before touchdown the same nozzles fire again as a landing burn, the same trick a Falcon 9 booster uses, so the suspension sees a 1 m/s landing instead of 8 m/s.

Staying level

Four attitude nozzles at the corners push the nose up or down. A feedback controller compares the car's pitch with the target and fires the right pair, the electric "micro adjustments" Tesla described. Try the 360° flip: the corner nozzles can't turn a two-tonne car fast enough without a bigger burn, and the landing burn then runs short of pressure.

τ = I·(θ̈ref + Kp·(θref − θ) + Kd·(θ̇ref − θ̇)), |τ| ≤ 2·F_att·arm

Why tyres hit a wall

A tyre can only push as hard as it is pressed into the road, about 1.3 times the car's weight for sticky rubber. That caps a normal car near 1.3 g, which is roughly 2 seconds to 60 mph no matter how much motor power you add.

F_tyre ≤ μ·(m·g + Δp·A_fan)

Two ways round it

The suction fans pull the car into the tarmac, adding grip without adding mass. The rear thrusters skip the tyres entirely: they push on the air they throw backwards. Together they take the model under one second, in line with Tesla's claim for the Thrust trim.

a = (F_tyre + F_thrust − ½ρC_dA·v² − C_rr·N) / m

The controller climbs to the target height, holds, and starts a controlled descent once the tank nears the regulator pressure, keeping air in reserve to land.

Why hover is short

Holding a two-tonne car up takes about 21 kN. A cold-gas nozzle on air gets about 67 seconds of specific impulse, so it has to throw away roughly 30 kg of air every second. A 600 L tank at 310 bar holds about 200 kg, and only part of that comes out above the regulator pressure.

ṁ = F / (I_sp · g₀) ≈ 20,600 / (67 × 9.81) ≈ 31 kg/s

The tank gets very cold

As gas leaves, the gas left behind expands and cools. Modelled as adiabatic (no heat from the tank walls, the worst case), it falls far below freezing, and colder gas gives less thrust per kilogram. Real tanks warm the gas a little, so treat the hover time here as a floor.

p = p₀·(m/m₀)^γ, T = T₀·(m/m₀)^(γ−1)

Fan power against suction

The fan is the trick

Fans in the rear diffuser pull air out from under the car. With the underbody sealed by a narrow skirt gap, a pressure drop of 8 kPa over 3 m² gives 24 kN of suction, a bit more than the car's weight. On a ceiling that leaves a few kN pressing the tyres on, enough to hold.

F_suction = Δp · A, holds if N = F_suction + W·cos θ > 0 and μN ≥ W·|sin θ|

Where it is hardest

Upside down is not the worst angle. Around 130° to 150° the car's weight both pulls it away from the surface and tries to slide it, so grip runs out first there. The fans also pay for every millimetre of gap: leakage, and so power, rises with the gap and with the square root of the suction.

Q = C_d · gap · perimeter · √(2Δp/ρ), P_fan = Q·Δp / η

Squeezing air costs energy

Filling the tank means taking about 160 m³ of ordinary air and squeezing it into 600 litres. Even a perfect compressor needs the isothermal work below; real multi-stage compressors lose a lot more as heat. That energy comes from the same battery that drives the wheels.

W_min = m·R·T·ln(p₀ / p_atm)

What it means on the road

A single launch is cheap. A jump with a landing burn uses around half the tank. Holding to a few showpiece moves per drive, with a slow top-up while cruising, is the realistic way to live with it.

Cold gas, not a rocket engine

Nothing burns. The tank stores air at very high pressure; open a valve and it rushes out through a shaped nozzle, faster than sound, and the car is pushed the other way. It is the same kind of thruster SpaceX uses to steer Falcon 9 boosters, only fed with air instead of nitrogen.

F = C_F · p_c · A_t, ṁ = p_c · A_t / c*, I_sp = C_F · c* / g₀

With a 100 bar feed and an exit-to-throat area ratio of 8, the model gives a thrust coefficient of about 1.55 and a specific impulse near 67 s. A 13 kN nozzle needs a throat about 33 mm across.

Ten nozzles, three jobs

Tesla confirmed ten thrusters. This model splits them by job: two at the rear for acceleration, four under the floor for lift, and four at the corners to control pitch and roll. The Thrust trim ships with the two rear nozzles only; the invite-only Rocket trim gets the full set.
